Value Study

Value — how light or dark something is — matters more to a convincing painting than color choice. A painting with the wrong colors but right values reads correctly from across a room; a painting with perfect colors but muddled values falls apart. This page is your at-a-glance reference, plus a tool below for analyzing your own photos.

The 9-Step Value Scale
The standard scale artists use to talk about value, from Light Light (1) to Dark Dark (9). Squint at your subject and try to place what you see on this scale before mixing.
🌫️
The Squint Test
Squint hard at your subject (or a photo of it) until detail and color blur away. What's left is pure value — the light and dark shape relationships that actually hold a painting together. Do this before you mix a single wash.
⚖️
The 60-30-10 Rule
Strong paintings usually favor one value family. Aim for roughly 60% one value range (light, mid, or dark), 30% a second, and 10% the third as accent. Equal thirds of everything tends to feel flat and unresolved.
🎯
Three Values First
Before worrying about all nine steps, block in just three: your lightest lights, a middle value, and your darkest darks. Almost every successful painting reads correctly at this simplified stage — refine from there.
💧
Watercolor Dries Lighter
Wet watercolor reads roughly 30-50% darker than its dry value. If a wash looks "right" while wet, it will likely dry too light. Mix and judge darker than feels comfortable.
☀️
Light Source = One Value Key
Everything lit by the same light source should share a value logic: sunlit areas cluster light, shadow areas cluster dark, regardless of local color. A red apple in shadow can be darker than a grey rock in sun.
🕳️
Save Your Whites
In watercolor, white is the paper — there's no white paint to add back later. Plan your lightest lights before you start, and paint around them deliberately from the very first wash.
Common Value Mistakes
✕
Everything mid-value. Timid mixing keeps every shape close to the middle of the scale, so nothing pops and the painting reads as flat and grey regardless of color.
✕
Local color confused with value. A bright yellow and a deep blue can sit at the same value — treating "bright" as automatically "light" causes shapes to separate incorrectly.
✕
Too many equal darks. Scattering your darkest value across many unrelated shapes competes for attention. Reserve true darks for a small, deliberate area.
✕
Value shifts mid-object. Letting a single plane in consistent light drift across several values (from inconsistent water control) reads as noise rather than form.
